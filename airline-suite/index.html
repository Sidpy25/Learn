Airline Commercial Suite

Revenue optimisation, inventory control, pricing, network planning, schedule changes, ancillaries and sales, aircraft rotations and crew in one place, all working from the same demand, fares and schedule. RM sets bid prices and seat availability across the O&D network; inventory lets analysts take over flight-dates; pricing builds the fares RM sells; planning decides where each fleet's block hours earn the most; schedule changes are checked for rotations, connections and contribution before they reach aircraft and crew.

Settings

Demand to come = weight × historical pickup + (1 − weight) × booked ÷ booking-curve %. Variance-to-mean ratio 1 = Poisson; higher = more volatile demand.

Demand adjustments & overbooking

Seasonality and event uplift scale the demand still to come for this departure (e.g. 1.2 for peak season, +15% for an exhibition). Optimised overbooking keeps adding seats while the expected fare of one more booking exceeds the expected denied-boarding cost, using each leg's no-show rate. History for classes that closed early is unconstrained: enter the days-to-departure at which the class closed in history, and its demand is scaled up by the booking curve (max ×3).

Buy-down model

Price-oriented customers buy the cheapest open fare on their O&D. If it is closed, they pay more only within their willingness to pay; the share who sell up from the lowest fare flow to fare f is exp(−β(f/flow − 1)), with β = ln 2 ÷ (FRAT5 − 1). The forecast splits demand into product-oriented demand (stays in its class) and price-oriented demand (share × total, spread by the sell-up curve). With fare adjustment on, each class is valued at its marginal revenue: the fare minus the revenue lost from customers who would otherwise have paid more. This stops the "spiral-down" where low classes keep selling to customers who would have paid more. Typical values: 0.3–0.6 price-oriented share for fenced network carriers, higher for fenceless low-cost fares; FRAT5 1.5–3.

O&D fare products

Path = legs used, joined with >. Fare = net fare for the whole itinerary. Historical final = average final bookings on comparable departures.

Leg bid prices

Network EMSR = EMSR-b on each leg, valuing every itinerary at its fare minus the bid prices of its other legs, iterated until stable. Network LP = shadow price of leg capacity in the network linear programme. Leg-only = EMSR-b with distance-prorated fares (segment control).

Bid price by leg and method

EMSR seat-value curve

Value of each remaining seat on the leg, network-adjusted. Bid price = value of the last remaining seat.

Group request evaluator

Displacement = the expected revenue of the seats the group would take on every leg (the top of each leg's seat-value curve, from the last remaining seat downwards). Accept if the group pays at least that, per passenger; ancillary spend and the probability of group attrition are not included.

Inventory control

Every flight on every date in the horizon is controlled by the system unless an analyst rule takes over. Manual turns system optimisation off for the matching flights: the analyst opens or closes classes and caps seats; classes not mentioned are held at the current system value. Adjusted keeps optimisation on but moves the bid price (± % or amount) and can force classes open or closed. Rules are checked top to bottom; the first match wins.

The system recommendation comes from the RM network, which models one departure per leg (the Network & demand tab). Every flight and date on that sector uses the same bid price, remaining seats and class availability; analyst rules then differ by flight and date. To control each flight-date on its own forecast, load that departure's bookings and demand in the RM network.

Control rules

First matching active rule wins

Sectors use the same patterns as pricing (ALL, RUH-JED, RUH-*, *-CAI). Days of week: 1 = Monday … 7 = Sunday. Times are departure times in the schedule time reference. Class AUs are nested authorisations including seats already sold (e.g. M:150, K:120: M and everything below may hold 150 seats in total, K and below 120). Cabin AU replaces the system overbooking. Seat limits cap one class only and are not nested (e.g. K:10). A rule past its expiry date stops applying automatically.

AU (authorisation) is nested, as in Amadeus or Navitaire inventory: it is the total seats this class and every lower class may sell, including seats already sold. Availability for a class = the smallest of (AU − sold incl. lower) for that class and every class above it, and never more than cabin AU − total sold. Lowering M's AU therefore also limits K, V and E; raising a closed class's AU above its sold count opens it. Seat limit caps one class only and is not nested. Cabin AU replaces the system overbooking for this flight-date. Extra cabin seats only reach a class whose own AU (and the AUs above it) leave room, so to sell overbooked seats in Y, raise Y's AU too. Leave a field blank to keep the system value. Changes preview straight away; saving creates or updates a rule for just this flight number and date, at the top of the list.

Flight-dates

Monte Carlo revenue simulation

Each run draws demand still to come and applies each control method to the same customers. Product-oriented customers ask for their class; price-oriented customers buy the cheapest class available to them within their willingness to pay (the buy-down settings define the market). Run it with the price-oriented share at 0 and at a realistic value to see where fare adjustment earns its keep. Perfect hindsight = the best revenue possible had the run's demand been known in advance. EMSR methods use nested booking limits; the LP method uses fixed bid prices. No re-optimisation during a run, so a live system that re-optimises will do somewhat better.

Planning settings

Frequency elasticity: 0.3 means doubling frequency grows route demand by about 23% (20.3). Demand variability: day-to-day spread of demand per departure (0.35 is typical); it drives spill when flights fill.

Fleets

Cost per block hour = variable operating cost (fuel, maintenance, crew, navigation). Fee per departure = airport, handling and other per-flight charges. Weekly block-hour budget = aircraft × utilisation × 7.

Routes (round trips)

Frequencies are weekly round trips. Demand = passengers per week per direction at the current frequency. Net fare = average net revenue per passenger per direction (for connecting traffic, the prorated share). Allowed fleets: "all" or a comma list such as A320neo,A321neo.

Fleets

Value of one more aircraft = extra weekly contribution if the fleet had one more aircraft at the same utilisation. Divided by its weekly block hours it gives the value of an extra block hour: a guide for wet-lease, utilisation and maintenance-slot decisions.

Crew pairing & roster

Builds crew duties from the aircraft rotations, then rosters named crew for the week. These are configurable planning rules for scenario work, not a certified implementation of GACA or any other authority's flight-time limitations; validate against your approved rules before operational use.

Max flight duty period (FDP) = max for 1–2 sectors − reduction × (sectors − 2), never below the minimum. Rest before a duty is at least the minimum rest or the length of the previous duty, whichever is longer. Duties are split where the aircraft returns to the crew's home base.

How to benchmark against your RMS on real flights

  1. Pick a network slice (3–6 legs through one hub) and one departure per leg.
  2. Export each O&D-class's net fare, bookings on hand and historical final demand; load them in Data & scenarios.
  3. Enter the RMS bid price per leg in "Your RMS bid price"; the Bid prices tab shows the gap.
  4. Compare open/closed decisions per O&D-class with the RMS availability display, especially connections on tight legs.
  5. Repeat at 60, 30, 14 and 7 days out to see whether gaps come from forecasting or optimisation.
